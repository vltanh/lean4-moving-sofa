\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The moving sofa problem}

Let $L$ be the L-shaped hallway of unit width, the union of the horizontal side
$H_L=(-\infty,1]\times[0,1]$ and the vertical side $V_L=[0,1]\times(-\infty,1]$.
A \emph{moving sofa} is a closed connected planar set that can be moved rigidly
from the horizontal side to the vertical side without leaving $L$
(\cref{def:sofa}). For example, the unit square is a moving sofa of area $1$.
Moser~\cite{Moser66} asked for the largest area of a moving sofa.
Hammersley~\cite{Hammersley68} found a sofa of area $\pi/2+2/\pi=2.2074\ldots$.
Gerver~\cite{Gerver92} found one of area $2.21953\ldots$, bounded by 18 analytic
arcs and segments, and conjectured that it is optimal. Romik~\cite{Romik18}
derived Gerver's sofa $\Gs$ from differential equations that express the local
optimality of its sides, and described its boundary explicitly in terms of two
angles that solve transcendental equations (\cref{fig:intro},
Appendix~\ref{app:gerver}). Kallus and Romik~\cite{KallusRomik18} proved by a
computer-assisted method that every moving sofa has area at most $2.37$.
Baek~\cite{Baek} proved Gerver's conjecture: every moving sofa has area at most
$\abs\Gs=2.2195\ldots$, where $\abs X$ denotes the area (Lebesgue measure) of a
set $X\subset\R^2$.

\subsection{Uniqueness and stability}

We prove that Gerver's sofa is the only optimal sofa, up to a rotation and a
translation. We write $R_\theta$ for the counterclockwise rotation of the plane
by the angle $\theta$ about the origin.

\begin{theorem}[uniqueness of Gerver's sofa]\label{thm:main}
  Let $S$ be a moving sofa (\cref{def:sofa}) with $\abs S=\abs\Gs$. Then there
  are an angle $\theta$ and a vector $v$ such that
  \[
    R_\theta S+v=\Gs .
  \]
\end{theorem}

Equivalently, a moving sofa has maximum area if and only if it is a rotated and
translated copy of $\Gs$ (\cref{cor:all}).

Note that \cref{thm:main} gives an equality of sets. This is stronger than what
an equality of areas yields, which allows the two sets to differ by a set of
measure zero, such as a point or a segment.

The uniqueness is stable (\cref{sec:stability}): a moving sofa whose area is
$\eps$ less than $\abs\Gs$ lies, after a translation, within Hausdorff distance
$O(\sqrt\eps)$ of $\Gs$ (\cref{thm:stability}), and the exponent $1/2$ cannot
be improved (\cref{thm:stability-exponent}).

\subsection{Baek's proof of optimality}\label{sec:baek}

We recall the outline of Baek's proof~\cite{Baek}, since ours follows it.
Appendix~\ref{app:baek} illustrates the notions and the steps of this subsection
(\cref{fig:capniche,fig:placements,fig:balance,fig:turn,fig:mamikon}).

When a moving sofa is moved from the horizontal side to the vertical side, it is
rotated by a total angle $\omega$, its \emph{rotation angle}; in
\cref{fig:intro}, $\omega=\pi/2$. Baek's proof fixes the sofa and moves the
hallway around it. For each angle $t$ between $0$ and $\omega$, place a copy of
the hallway, rotated by $t$, so that its two outer walls touch the sofa; the
sofa lies inside this copy. The \emph{cap} $K$ is the set of points that lie in
the horizontal side of the hallway at the start, in its vertical side at the
end, and inside the outer walls of all the copies. \cref{fig:capniche} shows
this for Gerver's sofa, with one of the copies in grey: the cap is the union of
the blue and the orange regions. Since $K$ touches both outer walls of every
copy, placing the copies against $K$ instead of the sofa gives the same copies.
So the copies, and their inner walls, one unit inside the outer walls, depend
only on $K$ and $\omega$. The \emph{niche} $\cN(K)$ is the part of $K$ that, for
at least one copy, lies outside the copy, in the wedge between its two inner
walls (in \cref{fig:capniche}, the wedge below the orange dot, between the two
grey lines that meet there). The set $K\setminus\cN(K)$ thus consists of the
points of $K$ that lie inside every copy; a set of this form is a \emph{monotone
sofa}, and its area $\cA_\omega(K)=\abs K-\abs{\cN(K)}$ is the \emph{sofa area}
of $K$. Gerver's sofa is a monotone sofa; in \cref{fig:capniche} it is the blue
region, and its niche is the orange region.

Baek proves that $\Gs$ is optimal in four steps.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  \item Baek shows that, after a translation, every moving sofa $S$ with
  rotation angle $\omega$ lies in the monotone sofa $K\setminus\cN(K)$ of the
  cap $K$ of $S$. Baek also shows that this monotone sofa is again a moving sofa
  with rotation angle $\omega$. So the area of $S$ is at most $\cA_\omega(K)$,
  and the problem becomes the maximization of $\cA_\omega$ over caps with
  rotation angle $\omega$.
  \item For every $\omega$, Baek obtains a cap with the largest sofa area as a
  limit of polygonal caps, and shows that these polygonal caps are balanced.
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Fix a finite set of angles strictly between $0$ and $\omega$. Any
    placement of copies of the hallway at these angles, together with the start
    and the end, cuts out a polygonal cap and a polygonal niche
    (\cref{fig:placements}), so the placement can be chosen freely and
    determines the candidate sofa. By a compactness argument, Baek shows that
    some placement gives the largest sofa area; its cap is a \emph{maximum
    polygon cap}. Baek also shows that, as the sets of angles become dense, a
    limit of these caps gives a sofa with the largest area.
    \item Maximum polygon caps are \emph{balanced}: each slanted side of the cap
    lies on an outer wall of one of the copies, and it is as long as the side of
    the niche on the inner wall of the same copy parallel to it, since otherwise
    moving these two walls slightly outward or inward would increase the sofa
    area (Gerver's balancing argument; \cref{fig:balance}).
  \end{enumerate}
  \item From the balance, Baek shows two properties of this cap, for a sofa of
  large enough area (which Gerver's sofa is).
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item One may assume, without loss of generality, that the rotation angle is
    $\pi/2$: a rotated copy of the sofa $K\setminus\cN(K)$ of this cap, which
    has the same area, turns by the full right angle (\cref{fig:turn}).
    \item For the rotation angle $\pi/2$, the cap satisfies the
    \emph{injectivity condition}: the inner corner of the hallway, as seen from
    the sofa, moves steadily to the left and never crosses its own path (the
    dashed curve in \cref{fig:capniche}).
  \end{enumerate}
  \item Baek bounds the sofa area of every cap with the injectivity condition by
  a concave functional $\cQ$ of the cap. The functional $\cQ$ is built from
  areas swept by segments tangent to the boundary of the cap
  (\cref{fig:mamikon}). The largest value of $\cQ$ is attained at \emph{Gerver's
  cap}, the cap of $\Gs$, where $\cQ$ equals $\abs\Gs$.
\end{enumerate}

For a moving sofa $S$ of large enough area, with rotation angle $\omega$ and cap
$K_S$, the four steps give
\begin{multline}\label{eq:baek-chain}
  \abs S\overset{(1)}{\le}\cA_\omega(K_S)\overset{(2a)}{\le}\cA_\omega(K_\omega)
    \overset{(3a)}{\le}\cA_{\pi/2}(K_{\pi/2})\\
  \overset{(3b),(4)}{\le}\cQ(K_{\pi/2})\overset{(4)}{\le}\cQ(\KG)=\abs\Gs ,
\end{multline}
where $K_\omega$ and $K_{\pi/2}$ are Baek's caps of step (2) and $\KG$ is
Gerver's cap. Only the first two terms involve $S$.

\subsection{The strategy of the uniqueness proof}\label{sec:strategy-uniqueness}

Suppose now that $S$ has area $\abs\Gs$. Then every inequality in
\eqref{eq:baek-chain} is an equality, but this says only that $K_S$, $K_\omega$
and $K_{\pi/2}$ have the largest sofa area, and that $\cQ$ is largest at
$K_{\pi/2}$; it says nothing about the shape of $S$. To use the equality case of
$\cQ$ for $S$ itself, we need the properties of step (3) for the cap $K_S$,
which has the largest sofa area but need not be a limit of maximum polygon caps
as in step (2a).

Baek derives step (3) from the balance of the maximum polygon caps in step (2b).
We replace this balance by bounds that we prove for every cap $K_*$ with the
largest sofa area. We approximate $K_*$ by polygon caps $K_n$ that, in place of
the sofa area of step (2a), maximize the sofa area minus a penalty for straying
from $K_*$ (\cref{sec:selection}). The penalty measures, at finitely many
angles, how far the walls of the copies placed against $K_n$ are from those
placed against $K_*$. Moving one wall of $K_n$ as in step (2b) now also changes
the penalty, so $K_n$ is only almost balanced: each side of the cap exceeds the
corresponding side of the niche by at most a small error, and the weights of the
penalty are chosen so that these errors add up to $O(\dH(K_n,K_*))$, where $\dH$
is the Hausdorff distance (\cref{sec:variations}).

In the limit this gives two inequalities for $K_*$, the \emph{pinned bounds},
and these are all that Baek's argument for step (3a) uses of the balance: they
put a small triangle at the inner corner $O$ into the niche, which keeps the
sofa narrow enough to make the missing turn by $\pi/2-\omega$ inside the
horizontal side before its own movement (\cref{sec:right-angle}). This produces
a second monotone sofa $U$ of area $\abs\Gs$ with rotation angle $\pi/2$ that
contains a rigid image of $S$. For the cap $K_U$ of $U$, which also has the
largest sofa area, the same approximation bounds how much boundary $K_U$ has at
each angle by the distances from the outer corner of the hallway to the points
where $K_U$ touches its outer walls; from these \emph{curvature bounds}, Baek's
argument for step (3b) gives the injectivity condition (\cref{sec:injectivity}).

Now the chain runs through the cap $K_U$ of $U$ itself: as $U$ has area
$\abs\Gs$, we have $\abs\Gs=\abs U=\cA_{\pi/2}(K_U)$, and step (4), which
applies as $K_U$ satisfies the injectivity condition, gives
\[
  \cA_{\pi/2}(K_U)\le\cQ(K_U)\le\cQ(\KG)=\abs\Gs ,
\]
where $\KG$ is Gerver's cap.
So every inequality is an equality; in particular, $\cQ(K_U)=\cQ(\KG)$, so $\cQ$
is largest at $K_U$ as well as at $\KG$. Since $\cQ$ is concave, and strictly so
in the lengths of the tangent segments of \cref{fig:mamikon}, this forces the
tangent segments of $K_U$ and of $\KG$ to have the same lengths. Hence $K_U$ is
a horizontal translate of Gerver's cap, and $U$ of Gerver's sofa
(\cref{sec:equality}). Finally $\Gs$ is the closure of its interior, which turns
the inclusion of a rigid image of $S$ in $\Gs$ into an equality
(\cref{sec:recovery}).

\runin{A second proof of optimality.} The pinned bounds and the curvature
bounds hold for every cap with the largest sofa area, and their proofs do not
use the value $\abs\Gs$ of that area, which comes from Baek's theorem. Baek's
caps $K_\omega$ and $K_{\pi/2}$ of step~(2) have the largest sofa area, so these
bounds give them steps (3a) and (3b) without the balance of step~(2b), and the
chain \eqref{eq:baek-chain} proves Baek's theorem again (\cref{rem:second}). In
particular, a cap $K$ with rotation angle $\pi/2$ and the largest sofa area has
\begin{equation}\label{eq:second-chain}
  \abs\Gs=\cA_{\pi/2}(\KG)\le\cA_{\pi/2}(K)\le\cQ(K)\le\cQ(\KG)=\abs\Gs .
\end{equation}

\subsection{The strategy of the stability proof}\label{sec:strategy-stability}

Now let $S$ be a moving sofa with rotation angle $\omega$ and area
$\abs\Gs-\eps$, where the \emph{deficit} $\eps>0$ is small. For uniqueness, the
pinned and the curvature bounds let the chain \eqref{eq:baek-chain} run through
the cap $K_U$, which comes from the sofa itself. These bounds are proved only for
caps with the largest sofa area, and the caps of $S$ need not have it. For
stability, the chain must still run through a cap of $S$; as it starts at
$\abs S=\abs\Gs-\eps$ and ends at $\abs\Gs$, each of its inequalities is then an
equality up to $\eps$. We use the \emph{right-angle cap} $K$ of $S$: the cap
built as in \cref{sec:baek} with copies of the hallway at all angles from $0$ to
$\pi/2$, even when $\omega<\pi/2$. We change steps (3) and (4) of the chain, and
add a compactness argument.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \emph{Step (4) becomes quantitative.} We extend $\cQ$ to caps with
  rotation angle $\pi/2$ that need not satisfy the injectivity condition, among
  them every cap close to $\KG$. For such a cap $K$, the gap $\abs\Gs-\cQ(K)$ is
  a sum of nonnegative terms (\cref{prop:stab-certificate}): the rate at which
  $\cQ$ decreases when Gerver's cap is moved toward $K$, which is nonnegative as
  in Baek's proof that $\cQ$ is largest at $\KG$, and \emph{energies}, which
  measure how far the tangent segments of \cref{fig:mamikon} for $K$ are from
  those for $\KG$. So $\cQ(K)\le\abs\Gs$. The energy of the cap also bounds the
  distance from $K$ to a horizontal translate of $\KG$: the Hausdorff distance
  between them is at most $2.002\sqrt{\abs\Gs-\cQ(K)}$
  (\cref{thm:stability-cap}). For $\cQ(K)=\abs\Gs$, this makes $K$ a horizontal
  translate of $\KG$; the proof of uniqueness got this from the case of equality
  in the concavity of $\cQ$.
  \item \emph{Step (3) is not needed near Gerver's cap.} If $K$ is close to
  $\KG$, Baek's bound $\cA_{\pi/2}(K)\le\cQ(K)$ holds even without the
  injectivity condition (\cref{sec:stab-local}); this replaces step (3b).
  Instead of step (3a), the missing part of the turn costs area: if
  $\omega<\pi/2$, then
  \[
    \abs S\le\cA_{\pi/2}(K)-c\,(\pi/2-\omega)
  \]
  for a constant $c>0$ (\cref{sec:stab-angle}).
  \item \emph{Compactness brings $S$ near $\Gs$.} If moving sofas with deficits
  tending to $0$ stayed away from every translate of $\Gs$, a limit of
  translates of them would be a moving sofa of area $\abs\Gs$ that is not a
  translate of $\Gs$; but every moving sofa of area $\abs\Gs$ is a translate of
  $\Gs$ (\cref{cor:translate}). So, when $\eps$ is small, a translate of $S$ is
  close to $\Gs$, its right-angle cap $K$ is close to $\KG$, and $\omega$ is
  close to $\pi/2$ (\cref{sec:stab-entry}).
\end{enumerate}
For this translate of $S$, with $\psi=\pi/2-\omega$, the three items give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stab-chain}
  \abs\Gs-\eps=\abs S\overset{\text{(ii)}}{\le}\cA_{\pi/2}(K)-c\psi
    \overset{\text{(ii)}}{\le}\cQ(K)-c\psi\overset{\text{(i)}}{\le}\abs\Gs-c\psi .
\end{equation}
Hence $\psi\le\eps/c$ (\cref{thm:stability-angle}), and
$\abs\Gs-\cQ(K)\le\eps$, so that $K$ lies within Hausdorff distance
$2.002\sqrt\eps$ of a horizontal translate of $\KG$. From the cap,
\cref{sec:stab-recovery} passes to the sofa: this translate of $S$ lies within
Hausdorff distance $O(\sqrt\eps)$ of $\Gs$, and their symmetric difference has
area $O(\sqrt\eps)$. The exponent $1/2$ cannot be improved: removing a small
open disk of radius $r$ from the interior of $\Gs$ leaves a moving sofa of
deficit $\pi r^2$, at Hausdorff distance $r$ from $\Gs$ and at distance at least
$r$ from every rigid image of $\Gs$ (\cref{sec:stab-puncture}).

\runin{One certificate for the three results.} The two parts of item (i),
$\cQ(K)\le\abs\Gs$ and the bound on the distance from $K$ to a horizontal
translate of $\KG$, form one estimate for $\cQ$, the \emph{coercive
certificate} (\cref{thm:certificate}). We call it a certificate as in
optimization, where a certificate is a proof of a bound that can be checked term
by term, such as an identity that writes the gap as a sum of nonnegative terms;
item (i) gives such an identity. We call it coercive because the energy of the
cap also bounds the distance from $K$ to a horizontal translate of $\KG$, so
that a small gap forces a small distance. \Cref{sec:unified} derives the three
results from it. In the second proof of optimality, its first part replaces the
last inequality $\cQ(K)\le\cQ(\KG)$ of \eqref{eq:second-chain}. For a cap with
the largest sofa area, the gap is then $0$, and its second part makes the cap a
horizontal translate of $\KG$, which replaces the case of equality in the proof
of uniqueness. For the right-angle cap of a sofa of small deficit, its first part
gives the last inequality of \eqref{eq:stab-chain} and its second part the
distance bound; item (iii) uses the optimality and uniqueness that it gives.

\subsection{Formal verification and the use of AI}

The proofs, together with Baek's, are formalized in Lean~4 with
Mathlib~\cite{Lean4,Mathlib20}, in the repository~\cite{Companion}: every result
of~\cite{Baek} used here, with the corrections of
Appendix~\ref{app:corrections}, the proof of \cref{thm:main}, a second proof of
optimality (\cref{rem:second}), the stability theorems of \cref{sec:stability},
and the derivation of optimality, uniqueness and stability from one estimate
(\cref{sec:unified}). Appendix~\ref{app:lean} describes the formalization, gives
the formal statement of \cref{thm:main} and of the stability theorems, and lists
the formal counterpart of each result of this paper. The formalization of
Baek's theorem and of the proof of \cref{thm:main} in
\cref{sec:reduction,sec:selection,sec:variations,sec:right-angle,sec:injectivity,sec:equality,sec:recovery}
is registered in the Palomar registry, which checks mechanically that it proves
exactly the stated theorems, using only Lean's standard axioms, and uses AI to
check that each formal statement says the same as its informal version. A second
entry, prepared for submission, states the certificate of \cref{sec:unified}
with optimality, uniqueness and the stability theorems, all proved through the
certificate. The
formalization also proves that its definitions
agree with those of Google DeepMind's formal-conjectures
project~\cite{FormalConjectures}, whose statements of the moving sofa problem
were written and reviewed by people independently of this work. The project
lists the uniqueness of Gerver's sofa as an open problem, and the author has
opened a pull request there so that the project's maintainers can check this
solution.

Much of this work was done with AI systems. They wrote the first version of the
proof, of the formalization and of this text. The author has proofread and
edited the abstract and \cref{sec:intro}, except the remark at the end of
\cref{sec:strategy-uniqueness}, \cref{sec:strategy-stability} and the
sentences of \cref{sec:intro} that refer to them, which were written later, and
has not yet read the other sections. Appendix~\ref{app:ai} names the systems and says what was checked, and by whom.

\cref{sec:reduction,sec:selection,sec:variations,sec:right-angle,sec:injectivity,sec:equality,sec:recovery}
